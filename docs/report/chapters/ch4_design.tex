\chapter{Proposed Scheme}\label{ch:design}

This chapter describes the core of the scheme: registration, authentication, handover, detection,
revocation and identity reveal, then the mechanisms that make it cheap on the radio channel and robust
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms}) and its support for emergency vehicles (Section~\ref{sec:emergency}).
Together they are the scheme evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the entities. The guiding principle of the design is that
\emph{proofs are verified at the edge and the ledger stores only shared state}. An RSU verifies a
zero-knowledge proof locally in well under a millisecond, while a Fabric commit takes hundreds of
milliseconds (Section~\ref{sec:res-ledger}). So the ledger is read from a local, regularly
synchronised copy, written asynchronously, and never waited for while a safety message is handled.
The ledger stores:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the Merkle root of each registration epoch (never a per-vehicle record);
  \item service policies: which vehicle classes may use which service;
  \item authorization records keyed by pseudonym, so that the next RSU can accept a handover;
  \item misbehaviour reports with evidence hashes, and the revocation list;
  \item trace requests and the RSU organizations' approvals for identity reveal.
\end{itemize}

\section{Registration}

For each vehicle $v$ the TA generates $P$ pseudonyms (5 by default), each a fresh key pair
$(x_i, Y_i = x_i G)$ with a random 64-bit identifier $\mathit{pid}_i$, and the vehicle's class
(\textit{car}, \textit{truck} or \textit{emergency}). It builds the Merkle tree over the leaves of
all pseudonyms of all vehicles, in shuffled order so that positions do not reveal which pseudonyms
belong together, and gives each vehicle its keys and Merkle paths over a secure channel. It publishes
the root with its validity period on the ledger through the chaincode function
\texttt{RegisterRoot}. Finally it encrypts the vehicle's identity record (identity and list of
pseudonyms) and escrows the key with the RSU organizations as $t$-of-$n$ Shamir shares, with
$t = 2f+1$ for $n = 3f+1$ organizations (5 of 9 in the evaluated scenarios).

Figure~\ref{fig:auth-seq} shows the exchange.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Every RSU broadcasts a signed beacon every 100\,ms with its identity, a fresh nonce $n$ and a
  timestamp. A vehicle accepts only beacons from RSUs whose public keys it holds.
  \item The vehicle computes the Schnorr response for the current pseudonym with
  \[\mathit{ctx} = \mathsf{enc}(\texttt{"VANET-AUTH-v1"}, \mathit{rsu}, n, ts, \mathit{svc}, \mathit{pid}, \mathit{class})\]
  and sends the pseudonym, its public key, the Merkle path, the proof, the requested service and its
  class.
  \item The RSU checks that the nonce is one it issued recently and that this (nonce, pseudonym) pair
  was not used before, rebuilds the leaf and verifies the path against the current root from the
  ledger, verifies the proof, checks that the pseudonym is neither revoked nor quarantined, and
  evaluates the authorization predicate
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:access}
    \mathrm{Access}(v, \mathit{svc}) = 1 \iff \mathrm{Verify}(\pi, Y) = 1 \;\wedge\; \mathit{pid} \notin \mathit{RL}
      \;\wedge\; \mathit{class} \in \mathrm{Policy}(\mathit{svc}).
  \end{equation}
  \item If access is granted, the RSU signs a ticket over the pseudonym, its key, the service, the
  class, its own identity and an expiry 30\,s ahead, records the authorization on the ledger
  asynchronously, and sends a signed PREAUTH note over the backbone to its neighbouring RSUs.
\end{enumerate}
The RSU learns the pseudonym, the class and that the vehicle is registered, and nothing else: the
leaf of another vehicle of the same class looks the same, the proof is zero-knowledge, and the
Merkle path reveals only the leaf position, which is shuffled.

\section{Handover and Pseudonym Change}

When a vehicle approaches the edge of its RSU's range (80\,\% of the nominal 300\,m) and hears a
closer RSU, or loses its RSU, it sends a handover request with its ticket, signed with the ticket key
and bound to the new RSU's beacon nonce. The new RSU checks the issuing RSU's signature on the ticket,
the request signature and the shared authorization: either the authorization record on the ledger or,
if the commit has not reached its local copy yet, the PREAUTH note from the issuing RSU. A handover
needs no new proof. After three sessions a vehicle switches to its next pseudonym and authenticates
afresh, so that a pseudonym's lifetime stays short.

\section{Safety Messages}

Each BSM carries the pseudonym, the kinematic fields and the ticket, and is signed with the
pseudonym's secret key, whose public key is in the ticket. A receiver checks the ticket's RSU
signature, that the ticket's pseudonym matches, the BSM signature and the ticket expiry. The receiver
needs no certificate chain and no ledger access. Carrying the full ticket would make every BSM
about 330 bytes; Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms} replaces it by a digest and most signatures by TESLA MACs.

\section{Misbehaviour Detection and Reporting}\label{sec:detect}

Every RSU runs a detector on each authentic BSM against that pseudonym's recent history. Hard
plausibility rules flag positions beyond radio range, speeds above 60\,m/s, accelerations above the
physical limit, positions inconsistent with the claimed speed, impossible heading flips, replays and
message rates above 13 per second. Weak rules (sudden appearance close to the RSU, two pseudonyms at
the same place) count only in pairs. An RSU reports a pseudonym once $K = 3$ of its last $W = 10$
messages were flagged, with the hash of a flagged, \emph{signed} message as evidence, through the
chaincode function \texttt{ReportMisbehaviour}.

\section{Revocation and Threshold Identity Reveal}

The chaincode revokes a pseudonym automatically once two distinct RSUs have reported it. A single
report, perhaps a detector error, therefore has no effect. RSUs see the revocation through their
local ledger copy and reject the pseudonym's BSMs and logins. An RSU that reported a now-revoked
pseudonym opens a trace request on the ledger (\texttt{RequestTrace}), and the RSU organizations
approve it (\texttt{ApproveTrace}). Once $t$ approvals are recorded, each approving organization
releases its key share, the TA reconstructs the escrow key, decrypts the identity record and revokes
all of the vehicle's pseudonyms. The vehicle's next login attempt fails and it is shut out. No single
party can unmask a vehicle: the TA lacks the key, and fewer than $t$ RSU organizations hold too few
shares.

\textbf{Ledger state and rules.} The world state holds the Merkle roots, the policies
$\mathrm{Pol}(\mathit{svc})$, the authorization records, for every pseudonym $p$ the set
$\mathcal{R}(p)$ of RSUs that reported it (one report per RSU, stored under the key $(p, u)$), the
revocation set $\mathcal{V}$, and for every trace request $q$ the set of approving organizations
$A(q)$. With $u$ the calling RSU, the chaincode accepts an authorization record
(\texttt{RecordAuth}) and revokes and reveals according to
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{accept}(p, \mathit{svc}, c)
    &\iff \mathrm{role}(u) = \mathrm{RSU} \;\wedge\; p \notin \mathcal{V}
       \;\wedge\; c \in \mathrm{Pol}(\mathit{svc}), \label{eq:recordauth}\\
  \mathrm{revoke}(p) &\iff \textstyle\sum_{u \in \mathcal{R}(p)} w_u \ge K, \label{eq:revoke}\\
  q \text{ approved} &\iff |A(q)| \ge t. \label{eq:trace}
\end{align}
Each weight $w_u \in [0, 1]$ is 1 unless trust weighting has lowered it (Section~\ref{sec:trust}), so
by default \eqref{eq:revoke} is $|\mathcal{R}(p)| \ge K$ distinct RSUs. A revoked pseudonym enters
$\mathcal{V}$, as do all pseudonyms of a vehicle that the TA revokes after an approved trace. No chaincode function removes
a pseudonym from $\mathcal{V}$, so a revocation is permanent, and every change to $\mathcal{R}$,
$\mathcal{V}$ and $A$ is a signed transaction in the hash chain of Section~\ref{sec:bg-fabric}. An
RSU's decision to admit a vehicle (Section~\ref{sec:auth}) checks the same predicate as
\eqref{eq:recordauth} on its local copy of the state, which is why the ledger can stay off the login
path.

\section{Efficiency and Robustness Mechanisms}\label{sec:mechanisms}

The core scheme described so far would be costly on the radio channel: every BSM would
carry a full ticket and a signature of about 330 bytes, ten times a second, and vehicles would have
no revocation information at all, accepting messages from revoked senders until their tickets
expired. This section describes the mechanisms that complete the scheme. Table~\ref{tab:mechanisms}
lists them with what each improves.

\subsection{Smaller Messages: Digests, Compact Encoding, Implicit Certificates}

A vehicle sends its full ticket only every tenth BSM, after hearing a new RSU, and at least every
2\,s; the other BSMs carry a 16-byte ticket digest that receivers resolve from a cache. The compact
wire format packs the kinematics into 16 bytes and uses 64-byte BIP-340 signatures. With ECQV
(Section~\ref{sec:tesla-ecqv}) the RSU issues an implicit certificate instead of signing the ticket, which saves the
explicit key and signature. The certificate request point is bound into the proof context, so nobody
can swap it on the air and receive a certificate for someone else's pseudonym.

\subsection{Cheaper Authentication: TESLA with Adaptive Signing}

With TESLA a BSM carries a 16-byte MAC under the current chain key instead of a signature. Keys are
disclosed two 25\,ms intervals later, in the vehicle's next BSM or in a small key message, so a
receiver can accept the BSM after about 50\,ms. A signed anchor BSM carries the chain commitment. To
avoid the TESLA delay when it brings nothing, the vehicle signs every BSM while its channel is quiet
and switches to TESLA when the channel busy ratio exceeds 0.30 (and back below 0.20, with 1\,s of
hysteresis). Urgent BSMs, after braking harder than 3\,m/s$^2$, are always signed so that neighbours
can act on them at once.

\subsection{Fewer Messages: Dead Reckoning}

Receivers predict a vehicle's position from its last BSM by dead reckoning. The vehicle sends a BSM
only when that prediction drifts by more than 1\,m, its speed changes by more than 0.5\,m/s or its
heading by more than $4^\circ$, and at least once a second. This follows the generation rules of the
European cooperative awareness service~\cite{etsi302637}. On straight roads at constant speed most
BSMs become unnecessary, while every manoeuvre is still reported immediately.

\subsection{Fewer Handovers: RSU Clusters and Ticket Renewal}

RSUs are grouped into clusters of $2\times2$, and a ticket is valid at every RSU of its cluster, so
moving between them needs no messages at all. Before a ticket expires, the vehicle renews it with one
signed request instead of a new proof.

\subsection{Load-Triggered Batch Verification}

Schnorr signatures can be verified in batches with one multi-scalar multiplication~\cite{bellare1998batch}.
The measured speed-up is \R{ct}{batch_speedup_8}$\times$ for 8 signatures and
\R{ct}{batch_speedup_32}$\times$ for 32. Waiting to fill a batch would add latency, so batching is
load-triggered: a signature that finds the processor idle is verified at once, and only signatures
that arrive while it is busy are verified together, up to 32, the moment it is free. If a batch fails,
bisection finds the invalid signatures.

\subsection{Revocation that Reaches Vehicles}

\textbf{Revocation filter.} Every fifth beacon (2\,Hz) carries a Bloom filter (7 hash functions, about 1\,\% false positives) of the pseudonyms
revoked or quarantined within the last ticket lifetime. Vehicles drop BSMs from pseudonyms in the
filter of the last beacon they verified. False positives are possible but rare, and they only drop
a message.

\textbf{Fast quarantine.} An RSU that reports a pseudonym also sends a signed QUARANTINE note over
the backbone to its neighbouring RSUs. Once notes from $K = 2$ distinct RSUs arrive, they block the
pseudonym locally until the ledger revocation lands, which takes milliseconds instead of a ledger
commit.

\subsection{Trust-Weighted Reports}\label{sec:trust}

A compromised RSU could report honest vehicles. With trust weighting, the TA investigates every
traced pseudonym. In the simulation the ground truth stands in for the investigation. Each RSU that
reported it gets a weight
\begin{equation}
  w = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{2\,(\mathit{tp}+1)}{\mathit{tp}+\mathit{fp}+2}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathit{tp}$ and $\mathit{fp}$ count its confirmed and wrong reports. This is twice the expected
trust of the beta reputation system~\cite{josang2002beta}, capped at 1. Revocation then needs a
report weight of at least $K$ instead of $K$ reporters. An honest RSU keeps weight 1; an RSU with more
wrong than right reports loses influence. Weights are stored on the ledger through
\texttt{SetReporterWeight}.

\subsection{Signal-Strength Check}

An RSU estimates the sender's distance from the received power using the scenario's path-loss model
with 4\,dB log-normal shadowing. It flags a BSM whose claimed position disagrees with that estimate
by more than four standard deviations and by at least 100\,m. A position-forging attacker cannot
move its own transmitter, so its claims stop matching its signal.

\section{Emergency Vehicles}\label{sec:emergency}

Emergency vehicles need the opposite of anonymity on one point: their privileged status must be
verifiable by everyone, and must be impossible to fake. The extension builds this on the
authorization predicate (\ref{eq:access}).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Registration.} The TA registers an ambulance's pseudonyms with class
  \textit{emergency}. The class is in the Merkle leaf and in the proof context,
  so a car cannot claim it.
  \item \textbf{Service.} Ambulances log in for the service \texttt{emergency-priority}, whose ledger
  policy admits only class \textit{emergency}. Their tickets therefore carry, signed by the RSU, the
  class and the service.
  \item \textbf{Siren alerts.} With the siren on, every BSM of an emergency vehicle is signed (never
  TESLA, so receivers need not wait), carries the full ticket and a flag \texttt{ev} inside the
  signed payload, and is never suppressed by dead reckoning. Vehicles process alerts from up to
  300\,m, beyond the 100\,m safety radius of normal BSMs.
  \item \textbf{Verification.} A receiver honours the flag only if the RSU-signed class of the
  sender's ticket is \textit{emergency}; otherwise it rejects the message as a fake. A signed fake is
  itself proof of the lie: no detector judgment is involved. The first RSU that receives one reports
  the pseudonym at once with the message hash as evidence, and quarantine and revocation follow.
  \item \textbf{Priority.} Emergency logins, renewals and handovers, and the RSUs' answers to
  them, are sent on the 802.11p access category AC\_VI instead of AC\_BE, so they do not wait
  behind the queue of ordinary logins on the radio. Safety messages keep the highest category,
  AC\_VO. The RSU also verifies them ahead of its processing queue: such a login waits only for the
  job in progress. Because the claim is checked only by the verification itself, an RSU grants
  priority to at most five claims per second, so false claims cannot crowd out everyone else.
\end{enumerate}
The fake-emergency attacker of the evaluation tries one emergency login, which the policy denies,
and then sets the flag on all of its BSMs.

\section{Security Analysis}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Impersonation.} An accepting proof requires $x_i$ for a leaf in the registered tree
  (soundness of the Schnorr proof and collision resistance of the hash). A signed BSM requires the
  key in an RSU-signed ticket.
  \item \textbf{Replay.} A login proof is bound to the RSU, a nonce the RSU issued within the
  freshness window and the pseudonym, and each (nonce, pseudonym) pair is accepted once. BSMs carry a
  timestamp and sequence number that the detector checks; handover requests are bound to the new
  RSU's nonce.
  \item \textbf{Class and service escalation.} The class is part of the Merkle leaf and of the proof
  context, so claiming another class breaks verification; the policy check (\ref{eq:access}) then
  decides the service.
  \item \textbf{Anonymity.} RSUs and vehicles see pseudonyms only, and the proof is zero-knowledge.
  Linking pseudonyms of one vehicle requires its escrow record, which needs $t$ organizations.
  \item \textbf{Accountability.} Every revocation and every reveal is a ledger transaction signed by
  identified organizations, and the ledger is append-only.
  \item \textbf{Limitations.} The TA knows at registration which pseudonyms belong to which vehicle.
  The RSUs learn which pseudonym was where, and a global eavesdropper can link consecutive pseudonyms
  by position. Anonymous credentials and mix zones would address both; they are left to future work
  (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).
\end{itemize}
